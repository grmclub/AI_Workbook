"Stop Over-Explaining: The 3 S’s Rule For Projecting Authority" by Codie Sanchez (BigDeal Podcast):

Core Insight

Smart, high-achieving individuals often sabotage their authority not because their ideas are weak, but because of poor speech delivery. Audiences evaluate speakers based on two primary dimensions: warmth and competence. To project authority, perceived competence must be prioritized through clean, deliberate communication [00:50].
Key Communication Traps & How to Fix Them
1. Excessive Hedging

    The Problem: Using qualifiers like "I could be wrong, but..." or "I'm not sure, maybe..." drastically lowers perceived competence and authority [00:38]. Research shows that speakers who present arguments with certainty—even when factually incorrect—are often judged as more credible than hesitant speakers who are right [01:46].

    The Fix: State conclusions directly grounded in data or explicit confidence levels [03:33].

        Instead of: "I might be wrong, but I think we should cut this feature."

        Use: "Based on the data we have, cutting this feature is the right move." [03:33]

2. Over-Explaining (Low Processing Fluency)

    The Problem: Clarifying, restating, and over-illustrating signals that you either doubt your audience's intelligence or doubt that your idea can stand on its own [04:23]. Cognitive psychology shows that simple, easily processed ideas (high processing fluency) are judged as more truthful and intelligent [05:05].

    The Fix: Deliver the core message concisely, then pause and let silence work [05:30]. Trust the listener to ask follow-up questions if they need deeper details [05:37].

3. Speaking Too Fast Under Pressure

    The Problem: Anxiety triggers the sympathetic nervous system, causing speech rate to accelerate [07:34]. Listeners unconsciously associate rushed delivery with uncertainty or a desire to finish before being rejected [07:40].

    The Fix: Apply the 3 S’s Rule: Shorter, Slower, Stronger [08:41]. Slow down by ~20% on key points, insert micro-pauses, and land important statements deliberately [08:13].

4. Specifying Specs Instead of Telling a Story

    The Problem: Leading with complex features or technical bullet points makes information forgettable [10:02].

    The Fix: Structure presentations around a narrative arc: Status Quo → Problem → Bold Promise → Proof → Future [10:12]. (e.g., Steve Jobs pitching the original iPod as "1,000 songs in your pocket" rather than listing hard drive specifications) [04:16].

5. Complex Frameworks vs. Simple Demonstrations

    The Problem: Over-complicating ideas or hiding behind jargon reduces perceived capability [12:40]. Secure speakers simplify complexity; insecure speakers try to prove their intelligence by piling on layers [13:02].

    The Fix: Aim for radical clarity [13:16]. "If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough." [12:56]

6. Skipping Deliberate Practice

    The Problem: Assuming natural delivery comes without effort leads to rambling, losing timing, and panic [14:22].

    The Fix: Rehearse key presentations out loud multiple times [14:33]. Deliberate practice is what creates the illusion of effortless charisma and allows real-time adaptability [15:12].

7. Chronic Self-Deprecation

    The Problem: Downplaying achievements or joking about flaws undercuts credibility, especially with new contacts [15:32]. Studies on job interviews demonstrate that strategic self-promotion increases ratings of competence and hireability, whereas excessive modesty reduces perceived capability [16:05].

    The Fix: Accept compliments cleanly with a simple "Thank you" rather than deflecting or minimizing your work [17:27].

Pre-Speech Reset Checklist [18:35]

Before entering high-stakes meetings or presentations, run through these quick diagnostic questions:

    Am I hedging unnecessarily?
    Am I over-explaining?
    Am I rushing key points?
    Am I over-complicating the core idea?
    Am I landing statements confidently?
    Am I comfortable with silence?
