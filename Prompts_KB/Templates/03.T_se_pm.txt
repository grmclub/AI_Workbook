##simple project management templates for small business

1. The "One-Page" Project Charter

Use this to align your team on a new project before anyone starts working.

    Project Name: [Name]
    Goal: [What are we trying to achieve? Keep it to 1–2 sentences.]
    The "Why": [What problem does this solve for the business?]
    Key Deliverables: [What exactly will we have at the end?]
    Deadline: [Target completion date]
    Key Stakeholders: [Who needs to be involved/informed?]

2. Simple Task Tracker (The "Who-What-When" List)Whether you use Excel, Google Sheets, or a simple Notion page, this table is the backbone of small business operations.

=============================================================================================
Task Name                |Owner  |Status         |Priority  |Deadline  |Notes
=============================================================================================
Create Marketing Assets  |Jane   |In Progress    |High      |May 25    |Need brand guidelines
Update Pricing Page      |Mark   |Not Started    |Med       |May 28    |Waiting on finance


3. The "Weekly Sync" Agenda

Keep meetings short (15–20 minutes max). Don't use meetings for status updates—use them for blocking and tackling.

    Review: Quick check-in on the "Big Wins" from last week.
    Priorities: What are the 3 top priorities for this week?
    Blockers: Where are you stuck, and what do you need from the team to move forward?
    Action Items: Who is doing what by when? (Assign names and dates).

4. Post-Project "Keep-Stop-Start"

Don't let a project end without a quick 10-minute debrief. This is the fastest way to grow your business efficiency.

    KEEP: What worked well that we should continue doing?
    STOP: What caused friction or wasted time that we should avoid next time?
    START: What is one new thing we should try on the next project to be better?

Implementation Tips for Small Teams

    Keep it Digital: Use tools like Trello (for a visual Kanban board), Asana (for task lists), or Google Sheets (for free, shared tracking).

    Focus on the "Next Step": In small businesses, people get overwhelmed by the "big picture." Always break projects down into tasks that can be completed in less than 4 hours.

    Automate Where Possible: If you are using a spreadsheet, use "Conditional Formatting" to turn cells red if a deadline has passed. It provides an instant visual cue of what needs attention.

#simple software issue management templates for small business 

For a small business, software issue management should prioritize transparency and urgency over complex ticketing systems. You need a process that tells you exactly what is broken, how bad it is, and who is fixing it.
Here are three simple, effective templates to manage software issues without the overhead of enterprise software.

1. The "Issue Triage" Log (Simple Tracker)
Whether in a shared spreadsheet, Trello, or Notion, this is your "Source of Truth."

============================================================================================================
ID   |IssueSummary                     |Priority       |Status        |Assigned To     |Impact
============================================================================================================
#001 |Checkout button broken on Safari |P0 (Critical)  |In Progress   |Sarah Cannot    |process payments
#002 |Typo in "Contact Us" email       |P3 (Low)       |Backlog       |Remote Dev      |Cosmetic/Minor

    • P0 (Critical): System down, users cannot pay, or significant data loss. Fix immediately.
    • P1 (High): Major feature broken, but a workaround exists. Fix within 24 hours.
    • P2 (Medium): Frustrating but not blocking core business. Fix in next sprint.
    • P3 (Low): Minor UI tweaks, typos, or suggestions. Fix when time permits.


2. The "Bug Report" Template
When someone (a team member or a customer) finds an issue, use this template to prevent "back-and-forth" emails.
	Issue Title: [Clear, descriptive summary]
	1. Description: What is happening?
	2. Steps to Reproduce:
			1. Go to [Page/Screen]
			2. Click on [Button/Link]
			3. Expected result: [What should happen]
			4. Actual result: [What actually happened]
			
	3. Environment: (e.g., Chrome/Safari, iPhone/Android, Desktop)
	4. Severity: [Critical/High/Medium/Low]
	5. Screenshots/Videos: [Link to file]		

3. The "Fix & Verify" Workflow
Keep your process linear to avoid confusion. For small teams, this 4-step flow works best:
    1. Reported (New): Issue is identified and added to the tracker.
    2. In Progress (Active): Someone is actively writing code to fix it.
    3. Testing (Verification): The fix is ready. Someone other than the developer checks to see if it actually works.
    4. Closed: The issue is resolved and pushed to the live site/app.

Best Practices for Small Teams
    • Centralize Everything: Pick one place (even if it's a simple Google Sheet) and make sure all issues go there. If it isn't in the sheet, it doesn't exist.
    • Avoid "Email Hell": If a bug comes in via email, copy-paste it into your tracker immediately. Do not use email to track the progress of a bug.
    • The "Reproduction" Rule: Developers cannot fix what they cannot recreate. Always insist on the Steps to Reproduce section; it saves hours of wasted investigation time.
    • Weekly Cleanup: Every Friday, spend 10 minutes looking at your "Backlog" (P3 issues). If something has been sitting there for three months, just delete it. If it were truly important, it would have been fixed by now.

1. Incident Management Workflow (DevOps/SRE Model)

Stage                    |Action
Detection & Alerting	 |Automated monitoring tools trigger alerts; incident is logged in the ITSM tool.
Triage & Priority	     |Severity is assessed (e.g., SEV1 to SEV4); on-call engineer is paged.
Investigation	         |Diagnostic data is gathered; communication channel (e.g., Slack/Teams) is opened.
Resolution	             |Fix is applied (deploy, rollback, or configuration change).
Validation	             |System is monitored to ensure the fix resolved the issue without side effects.
Post-Incident Review	 |"Blameless" review to document root cause and identify preventative actions.

2. Kanban Workflow (Operations & Maintenance)
Best for IT teams handling a mix of routine maintenance, small projects, and ad-hoc requests. It focuses on visual management and limiting Work-in-Progress (WIP).

Typical Kanban Column Structure:
    Backlog → Ready for Dev/Ops → In Progress (WIP Limit) → Testing/QA → Deployment → Done

    Key Operational Features:
        Swimlanes: Separate rows for different task types: Urgent/Incidents, Planned Projects, and Routine Maintenance.
        Fast Lane: A priority row at the top for "Stop-the-World" production issues that bypass standard queues.
        WIP Limits: Prevents "bottlenecks" by restricting the number of tasks allowed in the "In Progress" column.

##Requirements Scoping

--What is the difference between BRD and project plan?
Scope and Focus: BRD focuses on the high-level business needs and goals, while PRD delves into the technical details of implementing those requirements. 2. Timing: BRD is created during the initial stages of project planning, while the PRD is developed in the detailed planning and development phase.

--Why use a business requirements document template?
A business requirements document template gives you a consistent structure for every project, so you never miss critical information. Here's why teams rely on them:
    Stakeholder alignment: Define your project's boundaries and objectives upfront to keep everyone focused on the same goals.
    Scope control: A clear template helps prevent scope creep by documenting what's in and out of the project from the start, and supports a formal change control process.
    Time savings: Instead of starting from scratch, you have a proven structure that guides you through the process.
    Faster approvals: When executives and clients review a well-structured document, it's easier to get buy-in for your project plans.

--BRD vs. Similar Documents
	A BRD (Business Requirements Document) is a foundational project plan that defines a project’s goals, scope, and stakeholder expectations.
	
    vs. FRD (Functional Requirements Document): The BRD focuses on the why and what the business needs to achieve, while the FRD details the specific technical features and system behaviors needed to execute the BRD.
	
    vs. PRD (Product Requirements Document): The BRD is a high-level overview for business stakeholders, whereas the PRD is an execution-ready document tailored for engineering and design teams

--Key Components
A standard BRD typically includes:

    Executive Summary: A brief overview of the project and its purpose.
    Project Objectives: The specific business problems being solved and the desired outcomes.
    Scope Definition: The boundaries of the project, including what is included and what is out of scope.
    Stakeholder Mapping: Key people involved and their roles.
    Timeline & Milestones: Expected delivery dates and phases of work.
    Cost-Benefit Analysis: Financial impact, resources required, and potential ROI.
    Constraints & Risks: Potential obstacles, technological limits, and risks of inaction
	
The seven components of a BRD are:
    Executive summary
    Project objectives
    Project scope
    Business requirements
    Key stakeholders
    Project constraints
    Cost-benefit analysis

1. Executive summary

The executive summary is a high-level statement outlining what your project is and its purpose. Those who don't have time to read the BRD in its entirety should understand what you plan to accomplish by reading your executive summary.

2. Project objectives

Your project objectives are the business goals you want to achieve by putting your project into action. It's important to state your project objectives before kicking off any work so you can use them to measure your progress.

List your project objectives as SMART goals to ensure that they're:
    Specific
    Measurable
    Achievable
    Relevant
    Time-specific

3. Project scope

Your project scope communicates the boundaries of your project in your business requirements document. By defining your project scope in a scope management plan, you'll keep everyone on the same page and prevent scope creep, which is when your project expands outside of the boundaries you set for it and becomes hard to control.

Details to outline in your project scope include:
    Timeline
    Budget
    Deliverables
    Project requirements
    Project team
	
4. Business requirements

The business requirements are the meat of your BRD template. In this section, you'll list the actions required to accomplish your project. Depending on the project's complexity, this list may be just a few items or extensive.

In addition to listing your requirements and describing them, rank them by priority and assign each item an importance level based on its criticality. This will help others understand which requirements they need to complete first.

5. Key stakeholders

Project stakeholders include anyone with an interest in your project. These are likely the people who will read your BRD template to understand what the project is about. Your key stakeholders may be:
    Team members working on the project
    Project managers leading the project
    Executives approving the project
    Clients influenced by the finished project	
	
6. Project constraints

You likely presented an overview of your project constraints within your project scope, but here, you'll explain these boundaries in more detail. When the reader reviews this section, they should see the project's shape and its limits.

Project constraints may include:
    Project risks
    Team availability
    Resources
    Project dependencies
    Deadlines
    Project budget
	
7. Cost-benefit analysis

Ending your business requirements document with a cost-benefit analysis is a strategic move. If you're using your BRD to get approval for your project, this section may be the deciding factor. Clients and executives care about the project's objective, but if you can't prove you'll make a profit, all is lost.

To create a cost-benefit analysis:
    Describe all the costs associated with your project
    Explain the associated benefits
    Write the total expected cost of your project
    Estimate the expected ROI by subtracting your estimated costs from your estimated income
