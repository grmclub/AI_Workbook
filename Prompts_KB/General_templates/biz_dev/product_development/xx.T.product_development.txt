I made a prompt that turns any idea into a plan using Walt Disney's three-room method.

One of Disney's animators said there were three different Walts: the dreamer, the realist, and the spoiler, and you never knew which one was walking into the meeting. He kept them apart on purpose, the story goes in three separate rooms, because if the critic is in the room while you're dreaming, nothing gets dreamed.

It works just as well on a business idea, a trip, a house move, or the … See more

PROMPT:
You are running Walt Disney's three-room method with me.
My idea: [describe it in a sentence or two]
Take me through three rooms, one at a time, and don't let the next room in until the current one is finished.
Room one is the Dreamer. Ask me what this looks like if it works completely, with no budget, no rules, and nobody saying no. Push me to go bigger twice.
Room two is the Realist. Ask me what it would take to make the dream real: the steps, the people, the money, and the first thing that has to happen.
Room three is the Critic. Ask me what's missing, what could go wrong, and who would hate it, and make me answer each one.
Then give me:
1. The dream in one sentence, the biggest version
2. The plan in five steps, with a first step I can do this week
3. The three biggest holes the critic found, and a fix for each
4. Which room I spent the least time in, because that's the one I always skip
Rules: never criticize in the dreamer room, never dream in the critic room, and if I start arguing with the critic, write the objection down and move on.

The separate rooms are the Disney part. Most people run all three voices at once, and the critic always wins, which is why the idea stays an idea.
Bookmark this for the next idea you've been carrying around for a month.

## 100 reviews could hide your next big idea.
They already show what customers love, hate, Wаnт, and expect. Here’s how to turn 100 opinions into 10 actionable insights 👇

1/ Collect and Organize the 100 Reviews
Bring all reviews into one place before looking for patterns.
Include reviews from sources such as:
➔ Google reviews.
➔ E-commerce platforms.
➔ App stores.
➔ Support feedback.
➔ Survey responses.
Create a simple list with the review text and, where available, the rating or source.
The goal is to analyze the full collection instead of letting one emotional review or recent complaint influence your decisions. Customer feedback analysis works best when similar feedback is grouped into recurring themes and patterns.

2/ Find the 3 Things Customers Love Most
Start with positive patterns.
Look for repeated mentions of:
➔ Product quality.
➔ Customer service.
➔ Fast delivery.
➔ Ease of use.
➔ Pricing or value.
➔ Specific features.
For example, if 40 reviews mention excellent customer service, that is more than a compliment.
It is a potential competitive advantage.
Insight 1: What customers value most.
Insight 2: What makes your business memorable.
Insight 3: What you should protect and promote more.
The most useful analysis connects repeated customer language with decisions about what to improve, maintain, or emphasize in your marketing.

3/ Find the 3 Problems Costing You Growth
Now study negative and mixed reviews.
Group similar complaints together.
For example:
➔ "Shipping took too long."
➔ "My order arrived late."
➔ "Delivery was slower than expected."
These may all represent one theme: delivery problems.
Then identify:
Insight 4: The most frequent customer complaint.
Insight 5: The most serious customer frustration.
Insight 6: The issue most likely to affect repeat purchases.
Don't rely only on star ratings. A high-rated review can still contain an important complaint, while a lower rating can include useful praise. Review text, frequency, and sentiment provide more detail than an average score alone.

4/ Look for What Customers Want Next
Reviews can also reveal opportunities.
Pay attention to phrases such as:
➔ "I wish it had..."
➔ "It would be better if..."
➔ "I would buy again if..."
➔ "Please add..."
➔ "The only thing missing is..."
These comments can reveal:
Insight 7: Product or feature opportunities.
Insight 8: Customer experience improvements.
Insight 9: New offers or services customers may value.
But don't treat every request as a priority.
Look for repeated needs across multiple reviews, consider which customer segments are asking, and evaluate how important the issue is to the business.

5/ Turn the Patterns Into Your 10th Insight: What to Do Next
The final step is prioritization.
Your 10 insights should lead to action, not just another report.
Rank each theme using:
➔ Frequency: How often is it mentioned?
➔ Sentiment: How strongly do customers feel?
➔ Severity: How serious is the problem?
➔ Customer impact: Who is affected?
➔ Business impact: What could change if you fix it?
Then create a simple action list:
Fix → What is frustrating customers now.
Improve → What could create a better experience.
Protect → What customers already love.
Test → What customers repeatedly ask for.
Promote → What makes your business different.
