🚨 7 practical tips for differentiating learning to meet the needs of every student. 👇👇

1. Assess Readiness First
Before starting a lesson, find out what students already know, understand, and can do. Use quick questions, discussions, observations, or short pre-assessments to identify different readiness levels. Then use this information to adjust the level of challenge, support, and learning activities so students can build from their current understanding.

2. Focus on Essential Concepts
Identify the most important concepts, principles, and skills students need to understand. Build the lesson around these essentials rather than disconnected facts or activities. Students may take different learning paths, but they should all work toward the same core understandings.

3. Differentiate the Learning Process
Give students different ways to work with and make sense of the same essential concepts. Adjust activities, complexity, pacing, and support based on students’ readiness, interests, and learning profiles. The goal is not to give everyone the same task, but to provide appropriate pathways toward the same important learning goals.

4. Use Flexible Grouping
Organize students in different ways depending on the learning task. Use whole-class instruction, pairs, small groups, and individual work, and change the groups as students’ readiness, interests, and needs change. Avoid keeping students in fixed ability groups; flexible grouping gives them opportunities to work with different classmates and in different learning situations.

5. Offer Meaningful Choices
Give students appropriate choices in how they explore ideas, complete activities, or demonstrate their understanding. Choices can reflect students’ interests, strengths, and preferred ways of working while keeping everyone focused on the same essential learning goals.

6. Adjust Challenge and Support
Match the level of challenge and support to students’ readiness. Provide additional guidance, examples, or scaffolding when students need help, while offering greater complexity and independence to students who are ready for more challenge. Differentiation should change the quality and nature of the work, not simply give some students more or less work.

7. Use Multiple Ways to Show Learning
Allow students to demonstrate what they have learned in different ways. Depending on the learning goals, students might create a presentation, write a report, complete a project, solve a problem, or produce another appropriate product. Different formats can give students multiple ways to express understanding while still working toward the same essential learning goals.

##Claude can turn into a personal tutor that helps you learn anything 10x easier and faster
Here are 7 prompts you need to try👇

1/ Learn the basics fast
Prompt:
"I want to learn [topic]. Right now I’m a [beginner/intermediate], and my goal is to [goal, e.g. pass an exam or use it at work]. Show me the 20% of ideas that cover 80% of this subject, in the order I should learn them. For each one, give a one-line explanation, why it matters, and what I can skip for now. Finish with a quick test I can use to check I’ve got the basics down."

2/ Make it simple
Prompt:
"Explain [concept] in plain English, like I’m 4 years old. Skip the jargon, and if you must use a technical term, define it in one sentence. Give one real-world example that shows how it works. Then ask me to explain it back in my own words. When I do, tell me what I got right, what I missed, and what I got wrong, and keep going until I can explain it clearly on my own."

3/ Test myself
Prompt:
"Quiz me on [topic] to see what I actually remember. Ask one question at a time and wait for my answer before showing the correct one. Mix it up, multiple choice, short answer, and a few where I apply the idea to a new situation. Start easy, then get harder as I get them right. After 10 questions, give me my score, point out my weak spots, and tell me what to review first."

4/ Learn by questions
Prompt:
"Act as a patient Socratic tutor for [topic]. I’m trying to understand [specific question or problem]. Don’t just give me the answer, ask me one question at a time that nudges my thinking forward. If I’m wrong, ask a follow-up that helps me spot the mistake. If I’m stuck twice in a row, give me a small hint. At the end, sum up what I figured out in three short points."

5/ Never forget it
Prompt:
"Make me a 30-day study plan for [topic] using spaced repetition. I’ve got [X] minutes a day and I’m starting at [level]. Break it into small topics, then schedule each one to be first learned and reviewed after 1, 3, 7, 14, and 30 days. Each day should show what’s new, what to review, and a quick self-test. Lay it out as a table I can follow day by day."

6/ Compare to what I know
Prompt:
"I already understand [familiar subject] well. Explain [new topic] by comparing it to ideas from that subject. Start with the big picture, then walk through the 3-5 main parts of [new topic] and match each one to something I already know. Tell me clearly where each comparison breaks down, so I don’t pick up wrong ideas. Finish with two questions that test whether I understand the new topic on its own."

7/ Fix my mistakes
Prompt:
"Here’s my attempt at [problem/essay/summary/code]: [paste]. Review it like a strict but fair teacher. List each mistake, explain why it’s wrong, and show the correct version. Then tell me which mistakes point to a real gap in my understanding and which are just careless slips. Finally, give me a similar problem to try again, so I can check I’ve fixed the gap."
