Stop telling ChatGPT "summarize this"
Stop telling ChatGPT "explain this"
Stop telling ChatGPT "is this any good?"

That's how you get answers that sound smart and change nothing.

Use these 8 prompts instead 👇

1) The summary for a decision
"Summarize [DOCUMENT] for someone who has to decide [DECISION] by [DATE]. Lead with the three facts that change the decision, then anything that contradicts them, then what's missing. Under [WORD COUNT]. Skip every section that doesn't bear on this decision and tell me which ones you skipped."

2) The explanation at your level
"Explain [TOPIC] to someone who understands [WHAT I KNOW] but not [WHAT I DON'T]. Start with the one idea everything depends on. Use one example from [MY JOB]. After each part, give me the question I should now be able to answer, and wait for my answer before going on."

3) The honest feedback
"Here is [MY DRAFT]. It's for [AUDIENCE] and it has to [GOAL]. Don't tell me what's good. Tell me the three things most likely to make [AUDIENCE] stop reading, in order, with the exact line where each happens. Rewrite only the worst one. If it's fine, say so in one sentence."

4) The two-option comparison
"I'm choosing between [A] and [B] for [PURPOSE]. My constraints are [BUDGET, TIME, LOCATION] and I care most about [PRIORITY]. Compare them only where they differ. Tell me which you'd pick for me, the one condition that would make you switch, and the question I should answer before deciding."

5) The week plan
"Here's everything on my plate [LIST] and my fixed commitments [MEETINGS]. Pick the three that matter most for [MY GOAL THIS MONTH]. Give each a first step under 10 minutes and a day to do it. Park the rest on a later list. Then tell me which of the three I'm most likely to avoid, and why."

6) The hard email
"Reply to this email [PASTE]. They are my [BOSS / CLIENT / LANDLORD]. I need [OUTCOME]. I won't [LINE I WON'T CROSS]. Tone [FIRM / WARM]. Under [WORD COUNT], no apologizing for things that aren't my fault, end with one clear next step. Then tell me the line they'll push back on."

7) The learning plan
"I want to learn [SKILL] well enough to [WHAT I'LL DO WITH IT] within [TIMEFRAME], with [HOURS] a week. Give me the 20% that gets me 80% of the way, in order, one thing to practice per week, and a way to test myself each week that doesn't involve asking you."

8 ) The claim check
"Here's a claim I'm about to act on [CLAIM]. Split it into what's established, what's contested, and what's unknown. Mark anything you can't source as unverified. Give me the one check I can do myself in five minutes, then tell me plainly whether to act, wait, or verify first."

5. Use the Feynman technique
"Help me learn [TOPIC] with the Feynman technique. First, teach me the essential concepts briefly. Then ask me to explain the topic in my own words as if teaching a 12-year-old. Analyze my explanation, point out anything incorrect or unclear, reteach those gaps, and ask me to try again."

"Test my knowledge of [TOPIC] using active recall. Give me [NUMBER] questions one at a time, mixing definitions, explanations, applications, and real-world scenarios. [PASTE YOUR WORK]

8. Prepare me for an exam

## Mind Mapping: Improve Memory, Concentration, Communication, Organization, Creativity, and Time Management - Kam Knight

I used to take notes linearly. Page after page of sentences, bullet points, lists that stretched down the paper and disappeared into the margins. I'd finish a meeting or a lecture, look at my notes, and feel nothing but dread. The information was there, technically. But it wasn't alive. It didn't connect. It didn't stick. I'd review my notes before an exam or a presentation and realize I'd retained almost nothing.
Then I learned about mind mapping. And everything changed.
Kam Knight's book is the most comprehensive, practical, and visually rich guide to mind mapping I've ever encountered. It's not just a book about a technique. It's a book about a different way of thinking, one that works with your brain instead of against it.

5 Lessons That Stick With You:

1. Your brain doesn't think in straight lines.
Linear notes force your thoughts into a format your brain doesn't naturally use. Your mind works through association, connection, and radiance, one idea branching into another, then another. Mind mapping mirrors this natural architecture. That's why it works. It's not a gimmick. It's a translation.

2. A mind map is a picture of your thinking.
When you create a mind map, you're not just taking notes. You're externalizing your thought process. You can see how ideas relate. You can spot gaps. You can identify patterns you would have missed in a linear format. The map becomes a mirror for your own mind.

3. Memory improves when information is visual and spatial.
Your brain remembers images better than words. It remembers locations better than lists. Mind maps combine both, using colors, images, and spatial arrangement to encode information in multiple ways. That's why you remember longer. You're not just storing facts. You're storing a landscape.

4. Mind mapping works for everything.
Knight doesn't limit mind mapping to note-taking. He shows how to use it for brainstorming, planning, problem-solving, studying, writing, time management, and even parenting. It's a universal tool. Once you learn it, you'll find yourself reaching for it everywhere.

5. Children are natural mind mappers.
The book includes an in-depth discussion on mind mapping with children. Knight argues that kids are naturally good at this, they think in images, in connections, in bursts of imagination. Mind mapping taps into that natural ability instead of forcing them into linear formats that stifle creativity.
Mind Mapping by Kam Knight is not just a book about a technique. It's a book about a different way of being with information. It will change how you take notes, how you brainstorm, how you plan, how you study, and how you think.

It won't make you smarter. But it will help you use the intelligence you already have more effectively.
Read it if you're tired of forgetting what you learn. Read it if you're ready to think in a new way.
