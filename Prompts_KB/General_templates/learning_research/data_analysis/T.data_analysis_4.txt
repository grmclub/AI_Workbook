Here's my Ultimate Types of Data Analysis Cheatsheet:
There are 10 different types and knowing which one to use for which business problem is what separates a junior analyst from a senior one.

Descriptive Analytics
-- What happened?
-- Summarizes past data to track performance and trends
-- Used in dashboards, reports, KPIs

Diagnostic Analytics
-- Why did it happen?
-- Identifies root causes behind trends or issues
-- Drill down, segment data, find correlations

Predictive Analytics
-- What will happen?
-- Forecasts outcomes using data and models
-- Train models, evaluate, predict

Prescriptive Analytics
-- What should we do?
-- Recommends actions using predictions and optimization
-- Guides decision-making

Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)
-- What patterns exist?
-- Finds patterns, anomalies, and insights
-- Prepares data for analysis

Inferential Analytics
-- Can we generalize this?
-- Applies findings to larger populations
-- Uses hypothesis testing

Real-Time Analytics
-- What’s happening now?
-- Analyzes live data for instant decisions
-- Triggers alerts and dashboards

Qualitative Analytics
-- What do people think?
-- Analyzes text, feedback, and opinions
-- Finds themes and sentiment

Quantitative Analytics
-- What do numbers say?
-- Analyzes numerical data and metrics
-- Uses statistical methods

Causal Analytics
-- Does X cause Y?
-- Identifies cause-effect relationships
-- Uses experiments and validation

Here is what I see most analysts get wrong:
They jump straight to Predictive or Prescriptive analytics before they have mastered Descriptive and Diagnostic.
You cannot forecast what will happen if you do not first understand what already happened and why.

Start with Descriptive. Master Diagnostic. Then move up.
The analysts who get promoted are not the ones who built the most complex models.
They are the ones who picked the right type of analysis for the right business question — every single time.

## Exploratory Data Analysis: Understand the Data Before You Model It

EDA is where raw data starts turning into useful information. Before building models or drawing conclusions, you need to understand what the data contains, how variables behave, and what relationships or anomalies may exist.

A structured EDA process typically moves through:

• Data Understanding – inspect rows, columns, data types, summaries, and overall structure
• Univariate Analysis – study individual variables using distributions and descriptive statistics
• Bivariate Analysis – examine relationships between two variables through comparisons and correlations
• Multivariate Analysis – explore patterns and interactions involving several variables
• Insights & Reporting – translate findings into conclusions and meaningful visualizations

Important statistical measures include mean, median, mode, variance, standard deviation, IQR, skewness, and kurtosis.

Visualizations such as histograms, box plots, bar charts, scatter plots, correlation heatmaps, and pair plots help reveal patterns that summary numbers alone may not show.

With Python, libraries such as Pandas, NumPy, Matplotlib, and Seaborn can support the entire workflow, from initial inspection to deeper exploration.

EDA is not just about making charts. It helps you spot anomalies, understand distributions, discover relationships, and decide what should happen next in the analysis.

1. Day 1 · Build your first spreadsheet
Teach me Excel from zero using a [small online store]. Generate 30 fictional orders across [January–March 2025] in tab-separated format I can paste into cell A1. Include order ID, date, region, product, quantity, unit price, and status. Walk me through turning the data into an Excel Table named Orders, then have me calculate order value for one row and total completed sales. Show one worked row and wait for me to try the rest.

2. Day 2 · Clean messy order data
Give me a practice copy of my Orders table with one duplicate row, one missing quantity, inconsistent region labels, and dates stored in mixed formats. Keep a raw tab and guide me through fixing each issue on a clean tab. Have me flag the missing quantity for review and exclude that row from revenue calculations. Ask me to report the duplicate count, flagged row count, and clean row count before revealing yours.

3. Days 3–4 · Answer business questions with formulas
Use my cleaned Orders table to teach me SUMIFS and COUNTIFS. On Day 3, show one worked example of completed revenue for [East] in [February 2025], then ask me to repeat it for [West]. On Day 4, have me calculate completed order count and average completed order value using the same filters. Give me a small hand-check for each result and debug my formulas after I paste them.

4. Day 5 · Match tables with XLOOKUP
Create a five-row product cost table that matches most products in my Orders table and leaves one product unmatched for practice. Guide me through using XLOOKUP to bring in unit cost, flag missing costs, and calculate gross profit for completed orders with known costs. If I use [Excel 2019], teach me INDEX and MATCH instead. Give me one matched row to verify by hand, then review my formula.

5. Days 6–7 · Build a PivotTable and explain the result
Use my cleaned orders to teach me PivotTables. On Day 6, have me summarize completed revenue by month and region and reconcile the grand total to my formula from Excel. On Day 7, have me make a line chart and write two sentences identifying which region contributed most to the change from [February to March], with the dollar amount. Check whether my chart and totals support the conclusion.

6. Day 8 · Write your first SQL query
Teach me SQL from zero in [SQLite]. Give me a runnable script that creates and populates a small orders table using the same columns as my Excel exercise. Store dates as YYYY-MM-DD and use one row per order. Show me one SELECT example, then ask me to return the five highest-value completed orders using WHERE, ORDER BY, and LIMIT. Review my query and explain my first mistake.

7. Day 9 · Filter data without missing rows
Give me three SQL tasks using orders. Find completed orders in [February 2025], find orders from [East or West] worth at least [$100], and find rows with missing quantities. Include a date on the last day of February, a date on March 1, and one NULL quantity in the sample. After I try, check my date boundaries, AND/OR parentheses, and NULL handling.

8. Days 10–11 · Summarize sales with GROUP BY
Use my orders table to teach me GROUP BY. On Day 10, have me return completed order count, revenue, and average order value by region. On Day 11, have me return one row per month and region, then use HAVING to keep groups with at least [three] completed orders. Ask me to state what one output row represents and check that my totals reconcile before I add HAVING.

9. Day 12 · Join orders to customers
Extend my [SQLite] dataset with a customers table and a customer_id on each order. Include one customer with several completed orders and one customer with zero orders. Ask me to return every customer and their completed order count, including zero. After my attempt, explain why placing the order status filter in ON or WHERE changes the result and why counting order_id works here.

10. Days 13–14 · Answer a business question with SQL
Give me a two-day SQL challenge using my orders. On Day 13, have me calculate completed revenue by product for [February 2025]. On Day 14, have me use a CTE to find each product’s share of that month’s total revenue and return the top [three] products. Ask me to check that the shares add up to about 100% before limiting the results. Review my query after I submit it.

11. Day 15 · Describe a typical order
Give me 12 realistic completed order values in dollars, including one unusually large order. Have me calculate the mean, median, and interquartile range in Excel. Show me how to use QUARTILE. INC, then ask me to compare the results with and without the large order. Review which measure I’d report as the typical order value and why.

12. Day 16 · Spot sampling bias
Give me a scenario where my [online store] wants to estimate customer satisfaction. Offer three possible samples drawn from recent reviewers, repeat buyers, and randomly selected recent customers. Ask me who each sample could miss, how nonresponse might affect the result, and how I’d recruit [100] respondents. Review my plan and ask me one follow-up question about who the results would represent.

13. Days 17–18 · Calculate and explain a confidence interval

On Day 18, have me build an approximate 95% confidence interval and explain it in two plain-English sentences. Check when the approximation is reasonable and review my interpretation after I submit it.

14. Day 19 · Analyze a simple A/B test

15. Days 20–21 · Complete your first analysis project
Give me a two-day project using my online store data. If I’m starting fresh, generate a small dataset with two months and three regions. On Day 20, have me use SQL to find which regions contributed most to the change in completed revenue from [February to March], then verify the totals in Excel. On Day 21, have me make one chart and write a three-sentence summary using order count and median order value. Grade my calculations, chart, and conclusion, then give me one specific revision to make.
