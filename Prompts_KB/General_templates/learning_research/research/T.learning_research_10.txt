Use ChatGPT as a thinking partner, not just an answer machine.
These 20 prompts help you challenge bias, stress-test ideas, and reason with more clarity.


1/ 20 ChatGPT Prompts for Critical Thinking: Foundations
The first four prompts help you challenge how you are framing the problem before you commit to an answer.
➔ 1. First Principles Thinking: Give ChatGPT a [TOPIC]. Ask it to separate assumptions from verified facts, identify constraints and dependencies, rebuild the problem from fundamentals, and compare that result with conventional thinking.
➔ 2. Multi Perspective Analysis: Analyze the same topic through several viewpoints such as founder, customer, investor, expert, regulator, marketer, or engineer. Compare their goals, concerns, incentives, opportunities, and disagreements.
➔ 3. Cognitive Bias Detection: Give ChatGPT your [CONTENT]. Ask it to detect confirmation bias, anchoring, survivorship bias, sunk cost thinking, optimism, authority bias, framing, and loss aversion. Then examine how each bias affects the reasoning.
➔ 4. Devil's Advocate Stress Test: Give it an [IDEA] and ask it to attack the assumptions, logic, execution, timing, market fit, scalability, incentives, dependencies, and edge cases before recommending improvements.


2/ Better Decisions Before You Commit
These four prompts help when several options look reasonable but you need stronger reasoning before choosing one.
➔ 5. Decision Matrix Builder: Compare [OPTION A], [OPTION B], and [OPTION C] using criteria such as cost, effort, risk, upside, time, scalability, reversibility, learning, and strategic fit. Include best, average, worst, and black-swan scenarios before choosing.
➔ 6. Root Cause Analysis: Give ChatGPT a [PROBLEM]. Ask it to use Five Whys, causal chains, dependency mapping, and systems thinking to separate symptoms from root causes. Then rank possible fixes by impact versus effort.
➔ 7. Argument Strength Evaluator: Paste an [ARGUMENT]. Have ChatGPT break it into premises, evidence, assumptions, claims, and conclusions. Ask it to identify logical weaknesses, assess evidence quality, and rebuild the argument in a stronger form.
➔ 8. Scenario Planning: Analyze a [TOPIC / INDUSTRY / DECISION] across optimistic, realistic, pessimistic, and disruptive futures. For each, examine drivers, triggers, risks, opportunities, probabilities, and response strategies.


3/ Systems, Assumptions, Risks and Opportunity Costs
Good decisions become easier when ChatGPT helps you see what sits underneath the obvious answer.
➔ 9. Systems Thinking: Treat your [TOPIC / PROBLEM] as a system. Map inputs, outputs, actors, incentives, feedback loops, bottlenecks, dependencies, delays, and emergent effects. Then identify leverage points and unintended consequences.
➔ 10. Assumption Audit: Paste a plan or belief. Ask ChatGPT to extract every explicit and implicit assumption, classify them, estimate confidence, identify the assumptions supported by the weakest evidence, and explain what happens if they fail.
➔ 11. Red Team Analysis: Give ChatGPT a project, strategy, or decision. Ask it to search for vulnerabilities, blind spots, manipulation risks, execution failures, resource constraints, competitive attacks, human errors, and unexpected dependencies. Finish with mitigation actions.
➔ 12. Opportunity Cost Evaluation: Analyze a [DECISION] by looking beyond what you gain. Ask what you sacrifice in money, time, skills, relationships, growth, learning, optionality, and future positioning by not choosing the alternatives.

4/ Tradeoffs, Consequences, Evidence and Testing
These prompts are useful when a decision sounds good today but its long-term effects or evidence are unclear.
➔ 13. Strategic Tradeoff: Give ChatGPT a [CASE]. Ask it to compare short term versus long term, efficiency versus quality, speed versus accuracy, risk versus reward, growth versus stability, and simplicity versus flexibility. Then examine what happens when one variable is optimized at the expense of another.
➔ 14. Second Order Thinking: Give it a [DECISION]. Start with the immediate effects, then map secondary consequences, tertiary consequences, long-term ripple effects, unintended outcomes, and how different stakeholders could react over time.
➔ 15. Evidence Quality Analyzer: Paste your [DATA], claims, or sources. Ask ChatGPT to assess reliability, credibility, sample quality, bias risk, source independence, statistical validity, and limitations. Separate facts from interpretation and rank the evidence from weak to strong.
➔ 16. Hypothesis Testing: Provide a [HYPOTHESIS]. Define independent and dependent variables, assumptions, measurements, success metrics, and falsification criteria. Then identify supporting and opposing evidence and design tests that could strengthen or weaken confidence.

5/ Mental Models and the Full Critical Thinking Framework
The final four prompts help you combine several reasoning methods instead of relying on one way of looking at a problem.
➔ 17. Mental Model Stacking: Analyze a [TOPIC] through several mental models such as first principles, inversion, opportunity cost, Pareto principle, compounding, systems thinking, and game theory. Compare what each model reveals, then combine the strongest insights.
➔ 18. Inversion Thinking: Start with a [GOAL], but ask how you would guarantee failure instead of success. Identify the behaviors, assumptions, incentives, environments, and decisions that would create the worst outcome. Reverse those findings into principles for success.
➔ 19. Strategic Question Generator: Give ChatGPT a [PROJECT / PROBLEM]. Ask for deep questions covering assumptions, risks, opportunities, incentives, unintended consequences, ethics, scalability, timing, alternatives, evidence quality, uncertainty, and long-term effects.
➔ 20. Master Critical Thinking Framework: Analyze a [TOPIC] by defining the problem, validating assumptions, assessing evidence, checking biases, examining risks and alternatives, comparing tradeoffs, and mapping second-order effects. Finish with Facts, Assumptions, Risks, Opportunities, Contradictions, Unknowns, Strategic Options, and a recommendation with confidence level.
You do not need all 20 for every decision.
Pick two or three that match the problem you are working on and run them against something real. The goal is not simply faster output. It is better judgment before you act.

1. X-Ray the Whole Field in One Pass
Stop skimming abstracts one by one. This gives you the shape of an entire field in a single answer.

Prompt:

“Review all uploaded sources on [TOPIC]. List each one by author, year, title, and a one-sentence core claim. Then group them into major themes, show exactly where they agree or clash, and turn the theme groups into a comparison table I can export.”

Why it works: NotebookLM only answers from the sources you upload, so it will not invent a theme that isn’t actually there. Asking for a table forces it to compare instead of just listing.

Tip: Ask for the table in Markdown so it drops cleanly into a Doc, or push it to the new Data Tables output so you can send it straight to Sheets.

2. Expose Where the Experts Actually Disagree
Skip the boring summary. This shows you the fight happening underneath the field.

Prompt:

“Across all uploaded sources on [TOPIC], find the major contested claims. Split the authors into opposing camps, summarize each camp’s strongest argument, weigh the evidence for each side, and explain why the disagreement hasn’t been resolved yet.”

Why it works: Framing it as “camps” instead of “opinions” forces the model to actually take a side by side structure instead of a mushy overview.

Tip: Follow up with “which camp has the stronger methodology, not just the louder argument” to get past surface level disagreement.

3. Build a Living Timeline of a Key Idea
Great for intro chapters and defending why your question matters now.

Prompt:

“Identify the [3–5] most important concepts across the uploaded sources. For each one, find the earliest source that introduced it, explain how later sources expanded, refined, or challenged it, and lay it out as a dated timeline with citations.”

Why it works: Forcing a chronological structure stops the model from treating every source as equally important, which is what happens with a flat summary.

Tip: Once you have the timeline, ask NotebookLM to render it using one of the new infographic styles like Scientific or Editorial so you have a poster ready visual in minutes.

4. Hunt Down the Gaps Nobody Has Filled Yet
This is the prompt that finds your actual research question.

Prompt:

“Analyze the uploaded sources on [TOPIC] and identify the [3–10] most important research gaps. For each gap, explain why it remains unresolved, name the source that came closest to addressing it, recommend a research methodology to study it, and rank the gaps by potential impact.”

Why it works: Ranking by impact stops you from chasing a gap that’s unresolved simply because nobody cares about it.

Tip: Then ask “which of these gaps could I realistically study with the data or access I already have” to turn a wish list into a real project.

5. Turn a Pile of PDFs into One Clean Review
This is the one that actually writes your first draft.

Prompt:

“Using only the uploaded sources on [TOPIC], synthesize the literature by covering the main research questions, areas of consensus, major debates, well supported findings, current limitations, and the single most important unanswered question. Limit the response to [WORD LIMIT] words.”

Why it works: The word limit forces prioritization. Without it, the model will pad the answer with detail instead of judgment.

Tip: Turn this synthesis into an audio overview and use interactive mode to interrupt it with follow up questions while you’re walking or commuting.

6. Bulletproof Your Grant Application Before Reviewers Do
Catch your own weak spots before a panel does.

Prompt:

“Using my uploaded CV, publication list, and the attached guidelines for [GRANT NAME], draft the Lead Investigator section. Point out any gaps in my research track record, explain how a reviewer is likely to interpret them, and suggest realistic ways to strengthen the application before I submit.”

Why it works: Asking it to think like a reviewer, not like you, is what surfaces the blind spots you’re too close to see.

Tip: Paste in two or three funded proposals from the same scheme as extra sources so it can pattern match tone and structure, not just check boxes.

7. Turn Messy Feedback into a Clear Action Plan
For when three people gave you five contradictory notes.

Prompt:

“Analyze all uploaded supervisor, reviewer, or co-author feedback alongside my draft of [CHAPTER OR PAPER TITLE]. Identify the three most common criticisms, group similar comments together, prioritize the required revisions, and point to the exact sections or paragraphs that need updating.”

Why it works: Grouping comments by theme instead of by person stops you from treating every single note as a separate fire to put out.

Tip: Paste your own draft in full so it critiques your actual wording, not a generic version of your argument.

8. Turn a Conference Program into a Networking Game Plan
Walk in with a plan instead of wandering the hallway.

Prompt:

“Using the uploaded conference program and my research interests in [RESEARCH FIELD], build me a day-by-day schedule. Recommend the sessions most worth attending, name three researchers I should try to meet, explain why each one is relevant to my work, and write one short opening line I could use to start a conversation with each.”

Why it works: Naming specific people and giving you an opener removes the awkward blank stare moment at the poster session.

Tip: Upload the speakers’ recent abstracts as extra sources so the opening lines reference their actual current work, not just their bio.

9. Turn Academic Jargon into a Story Anyone Gets
For your family, your funder, or your own sanity.

Prompt:

“Using only the uploaded sources on [TOPIC], explain what the evidence supports most strongly, the biggest unanswered question, and the single most important real-world outcome. Write it like you’re explaining it to a smart friend outside the field. No jargon, no hedging.”

Why it works: Removing hedging language forces the model to commit to what actually matters instead of qualifying every sentence into mush.

Tip: Ask for a second version aimed at a 12 year old. If it can’t simplify further, you’ve probably found the real core finding.

📌 PRO TIPS
Upload full PDFs, not just pasted text or links. Docs, Slides, and Sheets update automatically when you edit them, but PDFs stay locked as a static snapshot, which is actually more reliable for citations.

Lock in a persona first. Use the Configure Chat feature to set a custom instruction so every single answer stays framed around your actual goal instead of drifting.

Wipe the slate between questions. Delete the chat history after a few exchanges so an old answer doesn’t quietly bias your next one.

One notebook, one topic. Mixing unrelated papers in the same notebook makes the AI blend things that should stay separate.

Teach it to say “not reported.” Explicitly ask it to write “not reported” instead of guessing when a source leaves a detail out.

Take control of Deep Research. You can now tell it to pull only from peer reviewed journals and official reports and to ignore blogs and opinion pieces entirely.

Export your tables, don’t just read them. Ask for a comparison table and push it straight into a spreadsheet for your appendix.

Make your timeline visual. Pick one of the ten new infographic styles to turn a concept timeline into something you could actually put on a poster.

Listen on the move. Turn a finished synthesis into an audio overview and interrupt it with follow up questions while you walk, cook, or commute.

🕵️ THE TRUTH
NotebookLM will not go out and discover every relevant paper for you.

It can now start from a rough idea and suggest sources, but you still have to check every single one.

It will not fabricate a citation out of thin air. If the answer isn’t in your uploaded sources, it will tell you that, not make something up.

But grounded does not mean flawless. Independent tests have caught real citation mistakes, wrong authors, mismatched page numbers.

So treat every output as a strong first draft, not a finished answer. Click through and verify before you trust it.

🚀 QUICK START
Create one notebook per research question, not one giant notebook per project.

Upload PDFs directly whenever you can instead of pasting text.

Turn on Configure Chat and paste in a short persona so answers stay on target from message one.

Run Prompt 1 first, always. Map the field before you try to argue with it.

Save your synthesis back into the notebook as a source so your next questions build on it instead of starting from zero.

💡 BOTTOM LINE
NotebookLM will not write your paper for you, and honestly, it shouldn’t. But it will read forty sources before your coffee gets cold and hand you the map, the fights, and the gaps, all with receipts attached. That’s hours back in your week, every single week.
