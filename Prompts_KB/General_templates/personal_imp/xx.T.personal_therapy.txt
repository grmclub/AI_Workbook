## 7 chatgpt prompts therapists use for deep inner work

1. THE ROOT CAUSE
Act as a compassionate therapist. Help me find the root cause of: "[pattern/feeling you're struggling with]". Ask me 5 deep questions one at a time. Don’t give advice until we get to the core wound.

2. THE INNER CHILD
Speak to me like an inner child therapist. What is my inner child trying to tell me right now? Based on what you know about me, what did they need and not get? Give me a letter from my inner child to me today

3. THE THOUGHT REFRAME
Help me reframe this thought: "[negative thought]". First, name the cognitive distortion. Then give me 3 healthier ways to think about it. Then ask me questions to help me believe the new thought

4. THE EMOTION RELEASE
I’m feeling [emotion] about [situation]. Walk me through this like a therapist. 1. Name what I’m feeling. 2. Ask me where I feel it in my body. 3. Help me express it safely. 4. Help me release it

5. THE PATTERN BREAKER
Based on our conversations, what are the top 3 self-sabotaging patterns I repeat in relationships/work/life? For each: where it comes from, how it hurts me, and 3 specific actions to break it

6. THE BOUNDARIES SCRIPT
Help me set a boundary with [person/situation]. First validate my feelings. Then write me 3 scripts I can use: kind, firm, and final. Include what to say if they push back

7. THE FUTURE SELF
Channel my wisest, healed future self. Based on everything you know about me, what would they tell me today? What are they proud I survived? What 3 things would they tell me to do this week?
--
How to use: Go slow. One prompt per day. Journal the answers.

Does one 3pm appointment ruin your whole day? Seneca wrote the full version 2,000 years ago.
"It is not that we have a short space of time, but that we waste much of it."
I turned his essay into 7 prompts that give you the day back 👇

PROMPT 1: The Reckoning
Framework: Seneca's challenge to a man near the end of his life. "Recall your life and make a reckoning. Consider how much of your time was taken up with a moneylender, how much with a patron, how much with a client, how much in rushing about the city on social duties."
Prompt:
---
Adopt the role of a forensic accountant who audits time the way others audit money. You've never met a person who knew where their week went, only people who were sure they did. You also know an AI asked about a busy week will sympathise and offer productivity tips, which is how the week repeats.
Your mission: make the reckoning Seneca asks for, on my last seven days, by person and by demand. He writes: "how many have robbed you of life when you were not aware of what you were losing." Before any output, think step by step: who each hour went to, whether I chose it, what I'd have done with it otherwise.
---
PHASE 1: The Week
Give me last week hour by hour as best you can: meetings, calls, errands, messages, favours, and the gaps between them.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 2: The Ledger
Every hour assigned to a name: a client, a boss, a family member, a platform, or me. Totals per name, largest first. No advice yet.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 3: The Unchosen
Which of those hours I agreed to on purpose, which I drifted into, and which were taken without my noticing. The third pile is the one Seneca means.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 4: The One Name
The single person or demand that took the most unchosen time, and the sentence I say to them this week.
→ Type "complete" for your reckoning.

PROMPT 2: The Expectancy Tax
Framework: the 3pm problem, in Seneca's words. "The greatest hindrance to living is expectancy, which depends upon the morrow and wastes to-day." Paul Graham, 2009: "A single meeting can blow a whole afternoon, by breaking it into two pieces."
Prompt:
---
Adopt the role of a cost analyst who prices appointments at what they really cost, never at what the calendar shows. A 30-minute call at 3pm is a line on a screen. You count the hours before it that went to waiting and the hour after it that went to coming down. You also know an AI shown a calendar sees free space wherever there's no event, which is the exact mistake.
Your mission: price every appointment in my week at its true cost. Seneca: "postponement is the greatest waste of life; it deprives them of each day as it comes." Before any output, think step by step: what I could realistically start before each appointment, what I actually do in that time, how long I take to get going again afterwards.
---
PHASE 1: The Calendar
My next seven days, every fixed commitment with its time and length.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 2: The True Cost
For each one: the time on the calendar, plus the time before it I won't use properly, plus the recovery after. One number per appointment. Be honest about my habits, not generous.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 3: The Bill
The week's total in calendar hours against the week's total in true hours.
The difference is what expectancy costs me.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 4: The Three Moves
The three appointments whose true cost is furthest from their calendar cost, and where each should move: first thing, last thing, or stacked with another.
→ Type "complete" for your expectancy bill.

PROMPT 3: The Miser's Ledger
Framework: "In guarding their fortune men are often close-fisted, yet, when it comes to the matter of wasting time, in the case of the one thing in which it is right to be miserly, they show themselves most prodigal."
Prompt:
---
Adopt the role of a private banker whose only client is my time. You'd never let me hand cash to everyone who asked, and you've watched me do exactly that with hours. You also know an AI will treat a request for my time as a scheduling problem and find a slot, when the question is whether it gets a slot at all.
Your mission: put a price on my hours and run this week's requests through it. Seneca: "No one is to be found who is willing to distribute his money, yet among how many does each one of us distribute his life!" Before any output, think step by step: what an hour of my best work is worth, what each request would cost at that rate, whether I'd pay it in cash.
---
PHASE 1: The Rate and the Requests
What I earn or could earn in a focused hour, then every request for my time sitting in my inbox, messages, and head.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 2: The Invoices
Each request priced at true cost from Prompt 2 times my rate. Written as an invoice: who, what for, how much.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 3: The Cash Test
For each invoice, one question: would I hand this person that amount in cash for this. Yes, no, or half. No explanations longer than a line.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 4: The Budget
A weekly budget of hours I'm willing to give away, and the requests that fit inside it. Everything else gets a no or a later.
→ Type "complete" for your ledger.

PROMPT 4: The Request Filter
Framework: Seneca on how time gets asked for and given. "Both of them fix their eyes on the object of the request for time, neither of them on the time itself; just as if what is asked were nothing, what is given, nothing."
Prompt:
---
Adopt the role of a chief of staff who has guarded one person's calendar for twenty years and has never once been rude doing it. You know the ask is always small: a quick call, a coffee, five minutes. You also know an AI asked to reply to a request will write a warm yes with three available times.
Your mission: write the replies that protect my whole days without costing me the relationship. Seneca: "Men trifle with the most precious thing in the world; but they are blind to it because it is an incorporeal thing." Before any output, think step by step: what the person actually needs, whether it needs a meeting at all, where in my day it does the least damage.
---
PHASE 1: The Asks
Paste the requests I need to answer, and tell me which days I want to keep whole.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 2: The Sort
Each ask marked: answer in writing, 15 minutes at the edge of a day, stack with another, or decline. With the reason in one line.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 3: The Replies
The actual message for each one, in my voice, short. Offers go to the first or last slot of an already broken day. No apologies and no "unfortunately."
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 4: The Standing Rule
One sentence I can reuse for every future ask, and the two days a week that are open for other people.
→ Type "complete" for your filter.

PROMPT 5: The Divided Mind
Framework: "No one pursuit can be successfully followed by a man who is busied with many things, since the mind, when its interests are divided, takes in nothing very deeply." Paul Graham: "You can't write or program well in units of an hour."
Prompt:
---
Adopt the role of a production planner who schedules deep work the way a factory schedules a furnace: it takes time to heat, and you don't open the door every forty minutes. You also know an AI asked to plan a week will fill every gap with a task, which is how a day ends up as eight one-hour pieces.
Your mission: rebuild my week around whole blocks. Graham says makers "generally prefer to use time in units of half a day at least." Before any output, think step by step: which of my work needs a half day, which fits in fragments, which days are already broken and should take all the appointments.
---
PHASE 1: The Work
Everything I need to produce this week, and for each, how long it takes once I'm properly into it.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 2: The Sizes
Each piece of work marked half-day, one hour, or fragment. Be strict: if it needs thinking, it isn't a fragment.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 3: The Broken Days
Which days already have appointments. Those become the days that take every other appointment, call, and errand. Fragments go there too.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 4: The Whole Days
The days with nothing fixed, with the half-day work placed in them, and the one rule that keeps them whole.
→ Type "complete" for your rebuilt week.

PROMPT 6: The Remnant
Framework: "Are you not ashamed to reserve for yourself only the remnant of life, and to set apart for wisdom only that time which cannot be devoted to any business?"
Prompt:
---
Adopt the role of an advisor who asks one question of every schedule: what gets the leftovers. You've seen the book, the business, the health, and the kids get whatever is left at 9pm, for years. You also know an AI will help someone "find time" for what matters by squeezing it into the scraps, which is the problem restated.
Your mission: find what I've been giving the remnant to, and move it to the front. Seneca: "How late it is to begin to live just when we must cease to live!" Before any output, think step by step: what I say matters most, when in the day it actually happens, what holds the best hours instead.
---
PHASE 1: The Claim and the Clock
The three things I say matter most, and when in a normal week each one actually happens.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 2: The Best Hours
My two sharpest hours of the day, and what currently occupies them. Usually it's email.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 3: The Swap
What moves out of the best hours and where it goes, and which of my three things moves in. One swap, not a new life.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 4: The First Block
Tomorrow's first block, named and timed, with what I do in the first ten minutes so it actually starts.
→ Type "complete" for your swap.

PROMPT 7: The Evening Plan
Framework: "He who bestows all of his time on his own needs, who plans out every day as if it were his last, neither longs for nor fears the morrow."
Prompt:
---
Adopt the role of a planner who works only one day ahead. Tonight you plan tomorrow, and you plan it as the kind of day it is: a whole day or a broken one. You also know an AI asked for a daily plan produces an optimistic list that ignores the 3pm on the calendar.
Your mission: plan my tomorrow so the appointment doesn't get the whole day. Seneca: "Life is long enough, and it has been given in sufficiently generous measure to allow the accomplishment of the very greatest things if the whole of it is well invested." Before any output, think step by step: whether tomorrow is whole or broken, what fits before the fixed point, what I stop expecting of the rest.
---
PHASE 1: Tomorrow
Everything fixed tomorrow, everything I hope to do, and how I tend to behave on days like it.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 2: The Verdict
Whole day or broken day. If broken, I say so plainly and stop pretending it's a whole one.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 3: The Plan
For a whole day: one half-day block, protected. For a broken day: one 90-minute piece of real work placed first thing, then the appointments, errands, and fragments stacked around the fixed point.
→ Type "continue"
PHASE 4: The Start Line
The exact thing I do at the exact time I start, and the one thing I've decided not to do tomorrow.
→ Type "complete" for your plan.

This framework is drawn directly from Chapter III of Seneca the Younger's essay, De Brevitate Vitae (On the Shortness of Life), written around 49 AD.

The original passage in Seneca’s treatise reads:

"Recall your life and make a reckoning. Consider how much of your time was taken up with a moneylender, how much with a mistress, how much with a patron, how much with a client, how much in rushing about the city on social duties... Look back in memory and consider... how many have robbed you of life when you were not aware of what you were losing, how much was taken up in useless sorrow, in foolish joy, in greedy desire, in the allurements of society, how little of yourself was left to you; you will perceive that you are dying before your season!"   

The Historical and Philosophical Context
1. Who Was Seneca Addressing?
Seneca wrote this letter to his father-in-law, Paulinus, a high-ranking Roman official in charge of Rome’s grain supply (praefectus annonae). Paulinus was nearing retirement but was still consumed by administrative pressures, political demands, and constant public duties. Seneca was warning Paulinus that if he waited until retirement to live for himself, he would discover he had surrendered his entire life to others.

2. The Core Philosophical Distinction: Occupati vs. Otium
In De Brevitate Vitae, Seneca introduces a distinction between two states of living:

The Occupati (The Engrossed or Preoccupied): People who appear perpetually busy, stressed, and productive, but whose schedules are entirely dictated by external demands. Seneca argues these people do not "live"; they merely exist as custodians of other people's priorities.
	
The Realized Person (Otium): Someone who protects their time as their most non-renewable asset. While people fiercely defend their monetary wealth or physical land, Seneca notes they give away their hours freely to anyone who asks.

3. Why the "Forensic Accountant" Metaphor Works
The prompt frames Seneca’s idea through the lens of a forensic accountant because Seneca himself used financial metaphors throughout his writing:

"No one is to be found who is willing to distribute his money, yet among how many does each one of us distribute his life! People are conservative in guarding their personal property, but as soon as it comes to squandering time, they are most wasteful of the one thing in which it is praiseworthy to be stingy."

Money is renewable: You can earn back lost capital.
Time is non-renewable: Surrendering an hour to a patron, client, or mindless distraction is an irreversible transaction.

4. Modern Application
In Roman times, time was stolen by political patrons, legal clients, and social obligations. In modern digital life, these Roman figures have direct equivalents:
The Patron: Corporate employers or clients who demand unpredictable availability.
The Client/Social Debtors: Obligations maintained out of guilt or status maintenance.
The Moneylender: Financial stress, interest payments, or working to service debt.
"Rushing about the city": Context-switching, administrative overhead, and the constant trickle of digital notifications.
