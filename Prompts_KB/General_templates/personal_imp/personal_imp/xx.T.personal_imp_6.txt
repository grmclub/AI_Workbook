Faster answers feel helpful, but better thinking is where the real value shows up.
Use it to challenge your ideas, not just write for you.
Here are 20 ways to do that 👇

1/ 20 ChatGPT Prompts for Critical Thinking: See the Problem Clearly
The quality of your decision depends on how accurately you understand the problem.
Use these methods before jumping into solutions:
➔ First Principles Thinking: Break the problem into basic facts. Separate what is known from what people merely assume.
➔ Multi Perspective Analysis: Examine the same issue from different viewpoints, such as the customer, competitor, employee, investor, or operator.
➔ Cognitive Bias Detection: Identify biases that may be shaping your judgment, including confirmation bias or overconfidence.
➔ Devil’s Advocate Stress Test: Challenge your strongest idea and look for reasons it could fail.
These approaches help you stop accepting the first explanation that sounds convincing.

2/ Find Causes, Test Logic, and Expose Weak Assumptions
Many bad decisions happen because people treat symptoms, assumptions, and confident opinions as facts.
Use these thinking methods to investigate more deeply:
➔ Decision Matrix Builder: Compare options using clear criteria instead of relying only on instinct.
➔ Root Cause Analysis: Trace the problem through causes, dependencies, and repeated "why" questions until you reach the underlying issue.
➔ Argument Strength Evaluator: Check whether a conclusion is supported by sound reasoning or simply presented confidently.
➔ Scenario Planning: Consider optimistic, realistic, pessimistic, and disruptive outcomes before committing.
➔ Systems Thinking: Look at how people, processes, incentives, resources, and consequences interact.
This helps you understand what is actually creating the result.

3/ Audit Evidence, Assumptions, and Tradeoffs
A plan can look strong while depending on weak evidence or fragile assumptions.
Use these methods to check the foundation:
➔ Assumption Audit: List the assumptions behind the plan and identify which ones could cause the biggest failure.
➔ Red Team Analysis: Search for risks, weaknesses, and blind spots that supportive teammates may hesitate to mention.
➔ Opportunity Cost Evaluation: Identify what you are giving up by choosing one option over another.
➔ Strategic Tradeoffs: Clarify what must be sacrificed to gain speed, quality, reach, profit, control, or simplicity.
➔ Evidence Quality Analyzer: Separate reliable evidence from opinions, incomplete data, and unsupported claims.
The goal is not to remove uncertainty. It is to understand exactly where uncertainty exists.

4/ Think Beyond the Immediate Result
A decision can solve today’s problem while creating a larger one later.
Use these methods to explore consequences and alternatives:
➔ Second Order Thinking: Ask what happens after the first result, then what happens after that.
➔ Hypothesis Testing: Turn a belief into something that can be tested rather than treating it as automatically true.
➔ Inversion Thinking: Instead of asking how to succeed, examine what would guarantee failure and avoid those conditions.
➔ Mental Model Switching: Analyze the issue through different frameworks instead of depending on one familiar way of thinking.
These methods are especially useful for pricing, hiring, product launches, marketing campaigns, partnerships, and major business decisions.

5/ Build a Complete Critical Thinking Process
Individual techniques are useful, but they become more powerful when combined into a repeatable decision process.
➔ Strategic Question Generator: Find the important questions that have not yet been asked.
➔ Master Critical Thinking Framework: Combine problem definition, assumptions, evidence, alternatives, risks, tradeoffs, and consequences into one structured review.
A practical sequence looks like this:
➔ Define what is actually true.
➔ Identify assumptions and biases.
➔ Examine multiple perspectives.
➔ Test the strength of the evidence.
➔ Compare available options.
➔ Map risks and future consequences.
➔ Choose based on judgment, not confidence alone.
ChatGPT is useful for producing output quickly.
But its bigger value is helping you notice the thinking errors that are difficult to see from inside your own head.

100 CEOs don’t chase inbоx zero.
They use simple systems to stop email stealing their focus.
The goal is protecting attention before the inbоx gets it.
Here are 7 habits worth stealing 👇

1/ Protect Your Priorities Before Email
Several leaders start their day by deciding what actually matters before opening their inbox.
Carter Reum writes down his priorities first. Claudia Romo Edelman identifies her "big rocks," while Bozoma Saint John spends time on reflection before checking email.
The lesson:
➔ Decide your priorities first.
➔ Then process incoming requests.
➔ Don't let other people's emails define your day.
Your inbox contains other people's priorities. Your calendar should reflect yours.

2/ Give Email a Time Slot
You don't need to check email every few minutes.
Rebecca Minkoff schedules 30-minute email blocks. Loren Castle uses "calendar stacking" to group similar work together. Lyft CEO David Risher checks urgent messages later and has another dedicated email period after dinner.
Try:
➔ Morning email block.
➔ Midday review.
➔ Final check when necessary.
The goal isn't a universal schedule.
It's giving email a defined place in your day instead of allowing it to interrupt everything else.

3/ Make Every Reply Shorter
Long emails create more work.
Siemens CEO Roland Busch receives around 200 emails daily and tries to keep his inbox below 100. Many of his replies are simply "OK" or "No."
Marc Jacobs CMO Kristin Patrick follows a three-line rule. If something requires a longer explanation, she prefers a quick call.
Use:
➔ "Approved."
➔ "Yes."
➔ "No."
➔ "Let's discuss."
➔ "Please send the details."
If the conversation becomes complicated, change the communication channel.

4/ Automate, Delegate & Set Boundaries
You don't need to personally process every message.
Klaviyo CEO Andrew Bialecki created AI scripts that screen his inbox and provide a consolidated summary. Naya CEO Hady Kfoury delegated customer-review emails and replaced a large daily stream with a weekly report.
Then create a stopping point.
➔ Filter before reading.
➔ Summarize before reviewing.
➔ Delegate repetitive information.
➔ Turn off unnecessary notifications.
➔ Set an evening cutoff.
The objective is to reserve your attention for decisions that actually require you.

5/ Know When Email Isn't the Right Tool
Some leaders manage email by using less of it.
Kevin O'Leary avoids email and prefers messaging for people who genuinely need him. Wolfgang Puck prefers calls for certain conversations. At Synthesia, internal communication happens through Slack, while external communication uses email and WhatsApp.
Even Mark Cuban takes the opposite approach, handling hundreds of emails daily with folders, multiple phones, and Gmail's suggested responses.
The bigger lesson:
➔ There is no perfect inbox system.
➔ Choose the system that fits your work.
➔ Protect your attention.
➔ Automate what doesn't need you.
➔ Use the fastest communication channel for the situation.
The goal isn't an empty inbox.
It's making sure your inbox doesn't become the person managing your day.
