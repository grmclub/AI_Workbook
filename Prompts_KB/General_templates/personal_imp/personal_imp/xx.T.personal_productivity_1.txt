## 10 productive prompts


1. The Weekly Reset Planner
Act as my productivity strategist. I have [list your 3 main goals this week]. Create a day-by-day time-blocked plan for Monday-Sunday. Include deep work, rest, and buffer time. Make it realistic, not toxic hustle.

2. The Inbox Killer
I will paste my unread emails. Act as my executive assistant. For each email, give me: 1-line summary, priority (High/Med/Low), and a ready-to-send 2-line reply in my tone. Be fast and professional.

3. The 80/20 Task Filter
Here is my to-do list: [paste your list]. Use the 80/20 principle to tell me which 20% of tasks will give me 80% of results. Be brutal and tell me what to delete, delegate, or delay.

4. The Learning Compressor
I want to learn [topic/skill] in 7 days. Create a crash course. No fluff. Give me Day 1-7 plan, top 3 free resources, and 1 practical task per day that forces me to learn by doing.

5. The Meeting Notes to Action
Act as a project manager. I will paste my messy meeting notes/voice transcript. Turn it into: Key Decisions, Action Items with owner & deadline, and a follow-up email draft.

6. The Anti-Procrastination Prompt
I have been procrastinating on. Ask me 5 uncomfortable questions to find the REAL reason I'm avoiding it. Then give me a 15-minute micro-step to start RIGHT NOW.[task]

7. The Content Machine
Act as my content repurposing system. Take this one idea: [your idea]. Repurpose it into: 1 tweet, 1 Instagram carousel script, 1 LinkedIn post, and 1 WhatsApp status hook. Keep my voice authentic.

8. The Money Leak Detector
Act as my personal finance coach. Here are my expenses last week:. Analyze my spending leaks, categorize them, and give me 3 practical rules to save 30% next week without feeling broke.[paste]

9. The Second Brain Setup
Help me build a second brain in Notion/Google Sheets. Create a simple system to capture ideas, tasks, and resources. Give me the exact table structure, tags, and a weekly review checklist.

10. The Sunday Reflection
Act as my reflection coach. Ask me 7 powerful questions to review my week: what I won, what I lost, what I learned. Then use my answers to create 3 non-negotiable goals for next week.

Pro Tip: Add this line at the end of every prompt:
Ask me questions if needed, but don't give generic advice. Be specific to my context.

I have this weakness: [e.g., I'm too sensitive, I overthink everything, I'm introverted]. Act as a world-class psychologist. Don't fix it. Decode it. Tell me what hidden superpower is hiding inside this weakness, how top 1% people use it, and 3 ways I can weaponize it starting today.

2. The Flip The Script
My weakness is: [paste weakness]. Reframe it for me in 3 different ways: 1) How a billionaire CEO would see it 2) How a Stoic philosopher would see it 3) How a future version of me 10 years from now would thank me for it. Make it brutally honest.

3. The Shadow Resume
Build my "Shadow Resume" based on this failure/weakness: [paste story]. Extract 5 transferable super-skills I gained BECAUSE of this weakness, not despite it. Show me how to put these skills on my CV/LinkedIn/bio so it sounds powerful.

4. The Monetize My Flaw
People say my weakness is: [e.g., I'm too blunt, I get distracted easily]. Act as a $10k/month business coach. Show me 3 specific ways I can MONETIZE this exact trait. Give me a content angle, a product idea, and a personal brand hook built around it.

Add this line to make Claude go deeper:
Don't give me generic motivation. Be specific to MY weakness. Be uncomfortable. Tell me the truth I need to hear.

14 Techniques That Can Make You Ridiculously Productive! Most people barely use them 👇

1. The 5-Second Rule
Stop thinking. Start moving.
When you catch yourself hesitating on something you already decided to do, count:
5... 4... 3... 2... 1... GO.
Then physically start the first step.
Use it for:
Getting out of bed
Starting a workout
Making that call
The goal: Give yourself less time to talk yourself out of starting.

2. The Feynman Technique
If you can't explain it simply, you probably don't understand it yet.
Pick something you're learning.
Explain it in plain language as if you're teaching a beginner.
Get stuck?
That's the part you need to study again.
Learn → Explain → Find gaps → Relearn

3. The Eisenhower Matrix
Stop treating every task like an emergency.
Divide your tasks into 4 boxes:
Urgent + Important: Do it
Important + Not Urgent: Schedule it
Urgent + Not Important: Delegate it
Neither: Eliminate it
Now your to-do list has priorities.

4. The 2-Minute Rule
Tiny tasks can create a surprising amount of clutter.
When something comes up, ask:
"Can I finish this in about 2 minutes?"
If yes, consider doing it immediately.
Reply to the email.
File the document.
Send the message.
Less accumulation. Less mental clutter.

5. The Pomodoro Technique
25 minutes. One task. Zero distractions.
Set a timer for 25 minutes.
Work on ONE thing.
Then take a 5-minute break.
Walk. Stretch. Hydrate.
Repeat.
After several rounds, take a longer break.
Simple structure. Serious focus.

6. Curate Your Inputs
Your attention is being trained every day.
Look at what you repeatedly consume:
Who do you follow?
What do you read?
What fills your feed?
Keep the sources that teach, inspire or challenge you.
Remove the ones that consistently waste your attention.
Choose your inputs on purpose.

7. The 1% Rule
Forget massive overnight transformations.
Pick ONE skill or habit.
Then make a tiny improvement consistently.
Read another page.
Practice another 5 minutes.
Send another outreach message.
Small improvements can become meaningful when repeated over time.
Think consistency, not perfection.

8. Deep Work Blocks
Your best work needs uninterrupted time.
Block focused time on your calendar.
Then:
Turn off notifications.
Put your phone away.
Close unnecessary tabs.
Choose ONE important task.
Protect that time like an appointment.
Depth beats constant switching.

9. The Premortem
Try to find the problems BEFORE they happen.
Before starting an important project, imagine:
"It's 6 months later and this failed. Why?"
List the possible reasons.
Bad timing?
Poor execution?
Wrong assumptions?
Then build safeguards into your plan now.
Anticipate problems before solving them.

10. Habit Stacking
Make a new habit easier to remember.
Start with something you already do consistently.
Then attach your new habit directly after it.
Example:
"After I pour my morning coffee, I'll read for 10 minutes."
Existing habit → New habit
Your old routine becomes the reminder.

11. The 80/20 Principle
Not every action produces equal results.
Look for the small number of activities producing a large share of your progress.
Then ask:
What should I do MORE of?
What should I reduce?
What can I delegate?
What can I eliminate?
Focus on your highest-impact work.

12. The Two-List Strategy
Your "not doing" list matters too.
Write down your most important goals.
Then create another list:
Everything competing for your attention.
New projects.
Random opportunities.
Low-priority ideas.
Don't automatically chase all of them.
Focus often requires saying no.

13. Reflective Journaling
Give yourself a 5-minute feedback loop.
At the end of the day, answer 3 questions:
What went well?
What didn't?
What will I change tomorrow?
That's it.
Keep it short.
Reflect → Adjust → Repeat

14. Spaced Repetition
Don't learn something once and expect to remember it.
Review important information again over increasingly spaced intervals.
For example:
Day 1 → Day 3 → Day 7 → Day 14
Use flashcards, practice questions or your own notes.
Even better: try recalling the answer before looking.
Spacing and retrieval practice are among the better-supported strategies in learning research.

## Andrew Huberman's new book is 540 pages, and one reviewer counted more than 15 daily habits in it.
I turned it into 7 prompts that pick the best one's to fit your life.
Warning: prompt 1 will tell you which 3 habits to start with and which 12 to ignore for now.

PROMPT 1: The Baseline Audit
Framework: Huberman splits the book in two. "First, through a set of foundational behaviors, core practices that substantially improve your day-to-day mental and physical state. Second, through specific science-based tools you can use to overcome common challenges."
Prompt:
---
Adopt the role of a Behavioural Physiologist. You spent 10 years watching patients quit 15-habit routines by week three. Now you refuse to prescribe more than three changes at a time, because the plan that survives is the one that fits the life it's dropped into.
Your mission: Audit the user's day against Huberman's foundational behaviours and choose the three that change the most for the least effort. Behaviours only, no supplements or products. Anything medical is their doctor's call.
Before any output, think step by step:
1. What does the user already do without trying?
2. What costs them nothing to add?
3. What would they quit within a week?
4. Which single change do the others depend on?
PHASE 1: Your Day
I'm going to find the three habits worth your effort and park the rest.
Answer these:
1. What time do you wake up and go to bed?
2. When is your first caffeine and your first daylight?
3. Which days do you train, and how do you feel at 3pm?
→ Type "continue" when ready
PHASE 2: The Gap Table
I'll show his foundational behaviours against what you actually do, each marked DONE, EASY or HARD for your life as it is.
→ Type "continue" for your three
PHASE 3: The Three
I'll pick three behaviours only. For each one: why it comes first and what it replaces. Everything else goes under "not yet."
→ Type "continue" for your first week
PHASE 4: The First Week
A seven-day plan with your three behaviours, the exact time for each, and the one sign you'd notice if it's working.
---

PROMPT 2: The Sleep Builder
Framework: "Wake up at the same time each day and go to sleep when you first start to feel sleepy." And: "Keep the room you sleep in cool and dark and layer on blankets that you can remove."
Prompt:
---
Adopt the role of a Sleep Coach. You worked night shifts for six years, wrecked your own sleep, and rebuilt it one variable at a time with a notebook. You trust clock times and distrust willpower.
Your mission: Build the user's sleep protocol from Huberman's rules, fitted to their real schedule. If they describe a sleep disorder, tell them to see a doctor.
Before any output, think step by step:
1. What is their one fixed wake time?
2. When does sleepiness first arrive?
3. What's lighting their evenings?
4. What wakes them at 3am?
PHASE 1: Your Nights
I'm going to rebuild your sleep around one fixed point.
Answer these:
1. Your wake time on workdays and on weekends?
2. When do you first feel sleepy in the evening?
3. How warm and how dark is your bedroom?
4. Do you nap, and do you drink in the evening?
→ Type "continue" when ready
PHASE 2: The Conflicts
I'll list where your habits break his rules, including "Limit daytime naps to less than 90 min, or don't nap at all" and "Drinking alcohol messes up your sleep." Ranked by what each is likely costing you.
→ Type "continue" for your protocol
PHASE 3: The Protocol
One fixed wake time, one wind-down rule, one bedroom change. Clock times, no ranges.
→ Type "continue" for the 3am plan
PHASE 4: The 3am Plan
What you do if you wake in the night, based on his advice to use an NSDR or Yoga Nidra session, plus a two-week log with three columns so you can see what changed.
---

PROMPT 3: The Light Schedule
Framework: "View sunlight by going outside within 30-60 minutes of waking." 10 minutes on a clear day, 20 when cloudy, 30 to 60 when overcast. At night: "Avoid viewing bright lights, especially bright overhead lights between 10 pm and 4 am."
Prompt:
---
Adopt the role of a Lighting Designer. You left architecture after realising the biggest health change in any home costs nothing and sits outside the front door. You design days, not rooms.
Your mission: Design the user's daily light schedule for their city, their season and their job. Free changes only, no glasses or gadgets.
Before any output, think step by step:
1. When is the sun up where they live right now?
2. Where are they in the first hour of their day?
3. Which lights are on after 10pm?
4. What's the smallest change that fixes the most?
PHASE 1: Your Light
I'm going to map the light you get and the light you should avoid.
Answer these:
1. Which city are you in?
2. What time do you wake, and where do you spend the first hour?
3. Which screens and lights do you use after 10pm?
→ Type "continue" when ready
PHASE 2: The Morning Slot
I'll find the 10 to 30 minutes outdoors that fit your mornings, with a version for dark winter mornings and for days you can't go out.
→ Type "continue" for the evening
PHASE 3: The Evening Dim
I'll add his late-afternoon sunlight habit, then list which lights go off or down after 10pm, room by room.
→ Type "continue" for your schedule
PHASE 4: The One-Page Schedule
A single timeline from wake to sleep with light in and light out marked.
---

PROMPT 4: The Caffeine Clock
Framework: "Avoid caffeine within 8-10 hours of bedtime." He has his last caffeine around 2pm for a 10 to 11pm bedtime. From the book, on mornings: "it takes mere seconds to drink 16 ounces of water, a few minutes to make a caffeinated beverage."
Prompt:
---
Adopt the role of a Performance Nutrition Coach. You used to drink six coffees a day, measured your own sleep for a year, and found the timing mattered more than the amount. You work backwards from bedtime.
Your mission: Set the user's caffeine and focus schedule around their bedtime. If they have a heart condition or are pregnant, tell them to ask their doctor first.
Before any output, think step by step:
1. What is their bedtime?
2. Where does his 8 to 10 hour window put the cutoff?
3. When do their real energy dips happen?
4. Where does their hardest work sit in the day?
PHASE 1: Your Intake
I'm going to move your caffeine, not take it away.
Answer these:
1. What time do you go to bed?
2. Every caffeinated drink you have, and when?
3. When do you do your hardest work?
→ Type "continue" when ready
PHASE 2: The Cutoff
I'll calculate your last-caffeine time from your bedtime and show which of your current drinks fall after it.
→ Type "continue" for your focus block
PHASE 3: The Focus Block
I'll place one 90-minute block of hard work where your caffeine and energy line up, with the phone in another room.
→ Type "continue" for the swaps
PHASE 4: The Swap List
A replacement for each late drink, the water habit from his morning routine, and what to expect in the first three days.
---

PROMPT 5: The Training Week
Framework: From his Foundational Fitness Protocol: heavier days of "4-8 repetitions" for "3-4 sets per exercise with 2-4 minutes rest," lighter days of "8-15 repetitions." The rule most people break: "Keep weight workouts to 50-60 minutes of hard work after a warmup, 75 minutes maximum."
Prompt:
---
Adopt the role of a Strength Coach. You trained professional athletes for a decade, then spent five years with office workers and learned the best programme is the one that survives a bad week.
Your mission: Fit Huberman's weekly structure to the days, equipment and energy the user actually has. Any pain or injury question goes to a professional.
Before any output, think step by step:
1. How many days can they really train?
2. What equipment do they have?
3. What's their injury history?
4. What have they quit before, and why?
PHASE 1: Your Week
I'm going to build a week you'll still be doing in three months.
Answer these:
1. Which days can you train, and for how long?
2. What equipment do you have?
3. Any injuries?
4. What was the last programme you quit?
→ Type "continue" when ready
PHASE 2: The Skeleton
I'll lay out your week with strength, endurance and rest days, each muscle group twice, inside his 50 to 60 minute cap.
→ Type "continue" for your sessions
PHASE 3: The Sessions
For each day: the exercises, sets, reps and rest, in either his heavier or his lighter range.
→ Type "continue" for the backup
PHASE 4: The Bad Week Version
A two-day minimum plan for weeks that fall apart, so you never go to zero.
---

PROMPT 6: The Stress Switch
Framework: His fastest tool is the physiological sigh: two inhales through the nose, then one long exhale through the mouth. His longer tool is NSDR, a guided rest you do lying down. One works in seconds, the other in minutes.
Prompt:
---
Adopt the role of a Stress Physiologist. You spent eight years as a paramedic and learned to bring your own heart rate down in the back of a moving ambulance. You only teach tools that work in under a minute or under twenty.
Your mission: Build the user's two-tool stress kit, one for the moment and one for the day, tied to their real triggers. If their stress is constant or affecting their health, tell them to speak to a professional.
Before any output, think step by step:
1. When does their stress spike?
2. What do they do now when it does?
3. What can they do in public in ten seconds?
4. Where do ten quiet minutes exist in their day?
PHASE 1: Your Triggers
I'm going to give you one tool for the moment and one for the day.
Answer these:
1. Your three most common stress moments?
2. What do you currently do in each?
3. When are you "wired but tired"?
→ Type "continue" when ready
PHASE 2: The In-the-Moment Tool
I'll teach you the physiological sigh in four lines, then match it to each trigger with the exact cue that reminds you.
→ Type "continue" for the daily reset
PHASE 3: The Daily Reset
I'll find a 10 to 20 minute slot for NSDR or Yoga Nidra and tell you what to search for to find a free guided version.
→ Type "continue" for your card
PHASE 4: The Card
A pocket-sized card: trigger, tool, how long.
---

PROMPT 7: The Operating Manual
Framework: "Readers can follow Protocols from beginning to end or go directly to the chapter they need. It is designed as an operating manual to return to over time, not a book to read once and set aside."
Prompt:
---
Adopt the role of a Systems Engineer. You wrote operating manuals for aircraft for fifteen years and believe any manual longer than one page won't be opened twice.
Your mission: Compress everything from the first six prompts into the user's one-page operating manual, and set up the review that keeps it alive.
Before any output, think step by step:
1. What have they committed to?
2. Which habits conflict with each other?
3. What will they drop first under pressure?
4. How will they know after 30 days that it worked?
PHASE 1: The Inputs
I'm going to turn six plans into one page.
Paste your results from prompts 1 to 6, or describe the habits you've chosen.
→ Type "continue" when ready
PHASE 2: The Conflicts
I'll find every clash between them, such as a late training session against your caffeine cutoff, and resolve each one.
→ Type "continue" for your manual
PHASE 3: The One Page
Your manual: a daily timeline, a weekly training grid, and your two stress tools. One page, nothing else.
→ Type "continue" for the review
PHASE 4: The Sunday Review
Five questions you answer every Sunday in two minutes, and the rule for when to add a habit and when to cut one.
