Here are five of the best AI prompt templates, designed to assist parents and educators:
1. The "Task Breakdown" Prompt (Executive Functioning)

Best for: Children who struggle with large, multi-step tasks (ADHD, autism, executive dysfunction).

    "I am helping a child with [diagnosis/need] complete [task, e.g., 'cleaning their bedroom' or 'writing a book report']. Please break this down into a step-by-step task analysis with no more than 3 simple steps per phase. Use short, concrete sentences and provide a way for the child to visually check off each step."

2. The "Social Story" Prompt (Social-Emotional Learning)

Best for: Preparing a child for new experiences, transitions, or social interactions.

    "Write a short, neuro-affirming social story for a [age]-year-old about [specific scenario, e.g., 'going to the dentist' or 'asking a friend to play']. Use simple, literal language. Avoid abstract metaphors. Include a section on 'what I can do if I feel overwhelmed' and end with a positive, predictable outcome."

3. The "Interest-Based Learning" Prompt (Engagement)

Best for: Improving focus and motivation by incorporating a child’s specific interests.

    "I need to teach a lesson on [topic, e.g., 'basic addition' or 'the water cycle'] to a [age]-year-old student who is obsessed with [specific interest, e.g., 'trains' or 'space']. Create a 10-minute activity plan that uses [interest] as the central theme to make the learning more engaging."

4. The "Simplified Explanation" Prompt (Communication)

Best for: Reducing cognitive load and helping children with learning disabilities or processing differences.

    "Rewrite the following paragraph to be suitable for a child with a reading level of [grade level/age]. Use simple vocabulary, avoid complex sentence structures, and explain the key concept in a concrete way. If there are any difficult words, provide a simple definition for each."
    [Paste complex text here]

5. The "Regulation & Calm-Down" Prompt (Self-Regulation)

Best for: Creating personalized coping strategies for when a child feels overstimulated.

    "Act as a special education consultant. Suggest 5 sensory-friendly calming strategies for a child who is currently experiencing [specific behavior, e.g., 'sensory overload' or 'frustration during homework']. Ensure the strategies are low-cost, require minimal materials, and focus on physical grounding techniques."

Important Guidelines for Using These Prompts:

    Privacy First: Never include a child’s full name, school details, or medical records in any AI prompt. Use generic identifiers like "a 7-year-old student."

    Human Oversight: AI cannot diagnose or replace professional therapy. Always use these prompts as a supplement to, not a replacement for, your child’s IEP (Individualized Education Program) or advice from specialists (BCBAs, OTs, SLPs).  

    Iterate: If the first result is too complex, add a follow-up prompt: "That is too much text. Can you simplify it and use bullet points instead?"

    Tailor for Strengths: Always frame the prompts around the child’s strengths. If they are visual learners, add: "Include a suggestion for a visual support or picture card that could accompany this activity."

##what are the essential life skills that can be taught to a 14 year old child in the austistic spectrum

Teaching life skills to a 14-year-old on the autism spectrum is most effective when the focus is on fostering independence, self-advocacy, and safety in ways that respect their unique neurodivergent identity.

Rather than trying to force neurotypical behaviors, neurodiversity-affirming approaches prioritize teaching skills that allow the teenager to navigate their environment with confidence, reduce anxiety, and express their own needs.  
1. Essential Skill Domains

Focusing on these areas provides a foundation for increasing autonomy as they move toward late adolescence:

    Self-Care & Hygiene: Routines for dental care, grooming, and personal cleanliness.  

        Tip: Use sensory-friendly products (e.g., unscented soap, tagless clothing) to ensure these tasks aren’t physically distressing.  

    Home Management: Basic tasks like doing laundry (sorting and washing), tidying a personal space, taking out the trash, and dishwashing.  

    Meal Preparation: Starting with low-risk tasks like pouring cereal or making a sandwich, then advancing to using a microwave or toaster. Always teach kitchen safety (e.g., using oven mitts, heat awareness).  

    Financial Literacy: Understanding the value of money, practicing counting cash, learning to use a debit card, and basic budgeting.  

    Community Navigation: Pedestrian safety (traffic signals, crosswalks) and eventually navigating public transportation using maps or apps.  

    Self-Advocacy & Communication: The ability to express boundaries, ask for help, or request a break when overwhelmed. This is a critical alternative to "social skills training" that focuses on masking or forcing eye contact.  

2. Neurodiversity-Affirming Teaching Strategies

The goal is to provide scaffolding—giving the support necessary for them to succeed, then gradually fading that support as they gain confidence.

    Break Down Tasks: Use "task analysis" to divide complex jobs into small, manageable steps. If they are learning to do laundry, start by just putting clothes in the hamper, then add steps over time.  

    Visual Supports: Use checklists, calendars, or photo sequences. These reduce the "cognitive load" of having to remember each step and can significantly lower anxiety by making expectations clear and predictable.  

    Use Special Interests: If they love a specific topic (e.g., space or trains), integrate it into the learning process. Use videos or books related to their interest to teach relevant skills.  

    Prioritize Regulation: Always teach them how to identify when they are overstimulated and provide safe ways to self-regulate (e.g., noise-canceling headphones, a designated quiet space).  

    Respect Authentic Communication: Focus on effective communication rather than "normal" social performance. Help them develop the tools to express their preferences and feelings authentically, which builds genuine confidence and mental well-being.  

3. Creating a Supportive Environment

    Consistency is Key: Practice skills at the same time and in the same way whenever possible. This creates a "predictable world" that reduces anxiety.  

    Scaffold, Don’t Fix: Resist the urge to jump in and complete the task for them. Allow them the time they need to complete it, even if it takes longer than it might for others.  

    Celebrate Small Wins: Focus on progress, not perfection. Every step toward independence is a major achievement.  

A Note on Approach

Be mindful of the difference between teaching a skill and "masking" training. A neurodiversity-affirming approach focuses on functional independence—helping your child live a life that is fulfilling and comfortable for them—rather than teaching them to hide their autistic traits or mimic neurotypical social standards to please others.

suggest 5 essential topics from school subjects that can be taught to a 14 year old child in the austistic spectrum

When teaching academic subjects to a 14-year-old on the autism spectrum, the most effective topics are those that bridge foundational knowledge with real-world application. For many autistic learners, abstract concepts are easier to grasp when they are tied to concrete, logical frameworks or personal interests.

Here are five essential academic topics that offer both educational value and practical utility:
1. Functional Math: Personal Budgeting & Time Management

Instead of abstract algebra, focus on the math required for independence.

    The Topic: Calculating prices with tax, managing a weekly allowance, and using digital or analog tools to manage a daily schedule.

    Why it works: It turns abstract numbers into tangible outcomes. It helps build a sense of agency over their daily life and reduces anxiety about upcoming tasks through better time tracking.

2. Science: Sensory Systems & Environmental Adaptations

Use their own biology to help them understand how their body interacts with the world.

    The Topic: Learning about the human nervous system, the five (or eight) senses, and how different animals or organisms adapt to their environments (e.g., how an octopus uses camouflage).

    Why it works: It provides a scientific, objective framework for their own sensory experiences. Understanding that "my nervous system is currently overstimulated" can be much more empowering than simply feeling overwhelmed without a name for the phenomenon.

3. Literacy & Social Studies: Media Literacy & Fact-Checking

At age 14, the ability to distinguish between objective facts and opinionated rhetoric is a vital life skill.

    The Topic: Analyzing news headlines, identifying logical fallacies, and learning how to verify sources.

    Why it works: Many autistic individuals have a natural affinity for systems, logic, and truth-seeking. Teaching them to "debug" a news story or a social media post can be highly engaging and provides a protective layer against misinformation.

4. Geography & Technology: Digital Mapping & Navigation

Move beyond rote memorization of capitals to practical navigation.

    The Topic: Learning how to read and use GPS tools, understanding how to plan a route on public transport, and the basics of how global connectivity works.

    Why it works: It provides a clear, logical structure for understanding the world. It also directly translates to increased physical independence, as they learn how to navigate their local neighborhood or city safely and confidently.

5. Social Studies: Civic Rights & Self-Advocacy

Focus on the history of rights movements to teach them about their own rights and boundaries.

    The Topic: Learning about human rights, the history of disability rights legislation (such as the ADA or equivalent local laws), and the concept of "reasonable accommodations."

    Why it works: It reframes the world as a place where they have a right to exist and thrive. Understanding their rights in school or future workplace environments is the ultimate tool for long-term self-advocacy.

Tips for Implementation:

    Leverage Special Interests: If the teen is interested in trains, use train schedules for Time Management. If they love animals, use that for Science/Biology.

    Keep it Concrete: Whenever possible, use visual aids, diagrams, and "real-world" examples rather than just textbook definitions.

    Focus on Autonomy: Always explain why this knowledge is useful for them personally. Autistic learners often find much higher motivation when they understand the functional purpose of a lesson.

suggest 5 essential topics from school physics that can be taught to a 14 year old child in the austistic spectrum 


Physics is an excellent subject for many autistic learners because it is built on predictable laws, logical systems, and clear cause-and-effect relationships. When teaching physics to a 14-year-old, focusing on observable phenomena allows them to connect abstract mathematical concepts to the world they experience every day.

Here are five essential physics topics that are highly practical and engaging:

1. Mechanics: Motion, Speed, and Velocity

This topic is the foundation of physics and relies on clear, logical calculations.

    Practical Application: Measuring the speed of a toy car, a bicycle, or a person walking. Use a stopwatch to calculate speed (v=d/t).

    Why it works: It is highly predictable and data-driven. It helps a teen understand the physics of movement, which can be useful for calculating travel times for public transportation—a key life skill for independence.

2. Electricity and Circuits: Practical Energy

Understanding how energy flows is both a science topic and a critical safety skill.

    Practical Application: Building simple circuits with batteries, LEDs, and switches. Discussing the path of electricity from a wall outlet to a device.

    Why it works: It provides a concrete, hands-on way to understand how systems work. It also teaches vital safety concepts (e.g., why water and electricity don’t mix, why frayed wires are dangerous), which helps with household independence.

3. Waves: Sound and Light

This topic explains how we perceive the world, which can be particularly relevant for those with sensory sensitivities.

    Practical Application: Using a tuning fork to visualize sound waves in water, or using a prism to split light into a spectrum.

    Why it works: It helps explain sensory inputs through an objective lens. Understanding that sound is a wave that can be blocked or dampened—and that light can be filtered—can give a teen a scientific framework for managing their own sensory environment (e.g., why noise-canceling headphones work).

4. Thermal Physics: Heat and Insulation

This is the physics of how objects gain, lose, and hold heat.

    Practical Application: Comparing how different materials (metal, plastic, wood, foam) hold heat. Testing how long it takes for a cup of water to cool down in different types of containers.

    Why it works: It is incredibly relevant to daily life (e.g., cooking safety, choosing the right clothes for the weather, energy efficiency in the home). It involves predictable, experimental results that are easy to measure with a thermometer.

5. Forces and Equilibrium: Gravity and Balance

This covers how things stand, move, and fall.

    Practical Application: Experimenting with center of gravity—stacking blocks or balancing objects. Learning how different surfaces (friction) affect whether an object stays put or slides.

    Why it works: It is tactile and physical. Understanding the concept of "equilibrium" (balance) can be very intuitive, and it explains the logic behind everything from how buildings stand up to how a car stays on the road.

Tips for Teaching Physics to an Autistic Teen:

    Prioritize Visuals and Hands-on Labs: Use simulations (like the PhET Interactive Simulations from the University of Colorado) to visualize invisible forces.

    Use Their Interests: If they love trains, study the physics of momentum and collision. If they love video games, explore the physics engines (gravity, velocity) used in their favorite game.

    Focus on the "Why": Autistic learners often thrive when they understand the systemic logic. Don’t just provide formulas; explain the physical mechanism—the "why"—behind the math.

    Relate to Sensory Needs: Whenever possible, link physics back to their personal experience. For example, explain "friction" by having them feel different surfaces, or "sound waves" by showing how they can "feel" a loud bass note.
